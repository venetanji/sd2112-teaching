## R1: rated responses are not a treatment trial

**Complete reference.** Hatch, S. G., Goodman, Z. T., Vowels, L., Hatch, H. D., Brown, A. L., Guttman, S., Le, Y., Bailey, B., Bailey, R. J., Esplin, C. R., Harris, S. M., Holt, D. P., Jr., McLaughlin, M., O'Connell, P., Rothman, K., Ritchie, L., Top, D. N., Jr., & Braithwaite, S. R. (2025). When ELIZA meets therapists: A Turing test for the heart and mind. *PLOS Mental Health, 2*(2), e0000145. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000145

**Correction.** Same authors (2025). Correction: When ELIZA meets therapists: A Turing test for the heart and mind. *PLOS Mental Health, 2*(8), e0000426. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000426

**Sources.** https://journals.plos.org/mentalhealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmen.0000145 ; https://journals.plos.org/mentalhealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmen.0000426

**Safe teaching claim.** In 18 couple-therapy vignettes, 830 US panel participants generally rated selected ChatGPT-4 responses higher on a brief perceived therapeutic-common-factors measure than selected responses from 13 experts. They identified human/AI authorship only slightly above chance. This tests judgments of selected written snapshots, not clinical effectiveness, actual therapeutic alliance, consciousness or genuine caring.

**Evidence locations.** Methods, “Knowing experts”: 13 experts, convenience sampled, 5–20+ years' experience; the authors selected the most-voted expert responses. “The GenAI” and prompt Table 1: ChatGPT-4, engineered definitions and ethics; AI responses also selected by the research team. “The panel”: N = 830; random assignment to a human or AI response for each vignette. “Common factors”: five self-report items, including “was caring and understanding”; not a patient-outcome measure. Results Aim 1: human identified correctly 56.1%, AI 51.2%. Results Aim 2: higher aggregate common-factor ratings for AI. Discussion immediately after Aim 2: “hypothetical ‘snapshot’ of therapy”; only 18 vignettes, a small clinician pool, one engineered prompt, no therapeutic context. AI responses were longer; do not treat prose style as isolated causally.

**Correction that must appear in notes.** August correction updates funding to internal Department of Psychology, Brigham Young University funds to SRB. It discloses SGH's ownership of Hatch Data and Mental Health and compensated therapy practice; that business did not fund the study and no clients were represented. ZTG/HDH held no financial interest or compensation in that business. The article's original “no specific funding/no competing interests” declarations are superseded. The correction does not itself report revised outcome data.

**Product inference, clearly marked as inference.** A caring-seeming response can shape expectations before actual treatment benefit has been established. Ask students what outcome would justify the word “therapy.”

## R2: disclosure, time, and the human comparator

**Complete reference.** Jain, G., Pareek, S., & Carlbring, P. (2024). Revealing the source: How awareness alters perceptions of AI and human-generated mental health responses. *Internet Interventions, 36*, 100745. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.invent.2024.100745

**Full text.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11090870/ (the correct PMC identifier).

**Safe teaching claim.** The initial 140-person assessment concealed AI origin; six months later 111 returned for a source-disclosed assessment of the same materials. Human response ratings increased while AI ratings changed little. At the disclosed assessment, humans were rated significantly higher for authenticity, but not professionalism or practicality. This illustrates that authorship context and rated content are distinguishable; it does not isolate disclosure from elapsed time, attrition or changing familiarity.

**Evidence locations.** Section 1.3 says initial respondents were under the impression all responses originated from human counsellors, and specifies Master's-level clinical-psychology students as the human writers. Sections 2.1–2.3: 140 initially, 111 six months later, same problems and responses; convenience/purposive sampling. Section 2.5 explicitly says anonymous responses made participant congruity difficult and “individual analyses were not conducted.” Results Table 1: authenticity 34.85 AI vs 37.66 human, d = .45, significant; professionalism 36.85 AI vs 35.83 human and practicality 36.24 AI vs 37.05 human are nonsignificant. Table 3 compares blind and informed groups: AI differences nonsignificant; human ratings increase in all three dimensions.

**Important source inconsistency.** A later Discussion sentence says humans were significantly higher in professionalism and practicality than ChatGPT. This conflicts with Table 1 and the Results text. Use Table 1's narrower finding, and do not repeat the overbroad discussion wording. Table 3 is about changes across assessments, not superiority over AI at the final assessment. Do not present nonsignificance as proof of equivalence.

**Product inference.** Test whether a source disclosure calibrates expectations in the team's actual context; do not infer that concealing the source is justified by high ratings.

## R6: immediate relief without demonstrated persistence

**Complete reference.** De Freitas, J., Oguz-Uguralp, Z., Uguralp, A. K., & Puntoni, S. (2026; first published online 2025). AI companions reduce loneliness. *Journal of Consumer Research, 52*(6), 1126–1148. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaf040

**Full published primary text hosted by author institution.** https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/AI%20Companions%20Reduce%20Loneliness%2011.7.2025_57451c02-8047-4e0d-abfc-55841f64166d.pdf

**Working link to author download.** https://www.hbs.edu/ris/download.aspx?name=AI%20Companions%20Reduce%20Loneliness%2011.7.2025.pdf (the register's /faculty/Pages/ route returned 403 in this audit).

**Version warning.** The final article is FIVE studies. The earlier HBS Working Paper 24-078 and arXiv 2407.19096 available in this audit have SIX studies, older author order, and different study numbering. Do not use their labels/sample details as final-publication details. Final publication author order is De Freitas, Zeliha Oguz-Uguralp, Ahmet Kaan Uguralp, Stefano Puntoni.

**Safe teaching claim.** In the final article's seven-day Study 3, AI companion interactions consistently reduced self-reported loneliness immediately after use. The study did not find sustained lowering of pre-interaction loneliness over the week relative to control. Feeling heard is a plausible studied mechanism, not evidence that the system feels care. App review findings (Study 1) are correlational; experimental evidence is separate.

**Evidence locations (PDF position = printed page in the author-hosted advance article).** p. 1 abstract identifies all five studies and p. 1 footer gives advance publication 25 June 2025. pp. 11–14 Study 3: repeated daily interaction with a prompted GPT-4 companion, control ratings without companion interaction; p. 13 Results: pre-session to post-session reduction each day. p. 14: “control versus before experience condition is not significant for all seven days (ps > .337).” p. 18 General Discussion: effects “did not accumulate or persist in the absence of interaction”; no sustained lowering across the week. p. 19 explicitly distinguishes temporary relief from enduring change and says it is not intended to inform clinical recommendations or replace human relationships/professional care. p. 15 notes differential attrition (23% experience, 14% control), completer analysis, propensity-matching and incomplete-case robustness checks. p. 20 outlines short duration, US participants and limited generalization.

**Do not draw an invented trend chart.** If using a simple original diagram, label it a conceptual distinction (during/after session versus between sessions), not observed numerical data.

## R7: a bridge toward human conversation, not proven better conversations

**Complete reference.** Maheshka, C., Folk, D., & Dunn, E. (2026). Harnessing artificial intelligence to facilitate difficult conversations. *Social Psychological and Personality Science*, advance online publication, article 19485506261444068. https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506261444068 (publisher date 5 May 2026).

**Primary author manuscript route.** https://dunn.psych.ubc.ca/curriculum-vitae-2/publications/ links https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/z0khdhugidncm3vca0hql/Maheshka-et-al..pdf?rlkey=l7y1pr20t8ays7solhsmplidd&dl=1 . This 27-page manuscript was reviewed in full; it is author-hosted/in-press text, not the publisher typeset article. The publisher endpoint returned 403.

**Safe teaching claim.** US/Canadian Prolific participants identified a difficult conversation, then were randomly assigned either 6–12 minutes preparing with GPT-4o-mini or asking it three neutral factual questions. At follow-up the next week, 65% of preparation participants versus 59% of controls reported having the conversation (N = 1,371 follow-up). This is a modest causal effect of this preparation package against this control on self-reported initiation, not proof of superior conversation quality or superiority over a human rehearsal partner.

**Evidence locations (PDF positions; printed pages are one lower after title page).** PDF p. 6 / printed p. 5: 1,465 completed Part 1; 1,371 returned; similar return rate by condition. PDF p. 7 / printed p. 6: randomization, preparation duration, neutral questions, GPT-4o-mini, April 2025, follow-up opens after five days for four days. PDF p. 10 / printed p. 9: 65% vs 59%, chi-squared p = .019, phi = .06. PDF p. 13 / printed p. 12: among those reporting having the conversation (N = 853), no significant condition differences in perceived quality, satisfaction, difficulty or thoroughness (p values .155–.816). PDF pp. 14–15 / printed pp. 13–14: control not matched for duration or prompt, no comparative efficacy test against human preparation/journaling; North American sample. Do not overinterpret null differences as equivalence. Proposed mediation through anticipated quality is not an experimentally isolated mechanism.

**Product inference.** A product can support a human relationship by helping someone prepare to act outside the app. Measure the external action as well as engagement.

## R8: source choice and response ratings are different outcomes

**Complete reference.** Wenger, J. D., Cameron, C. D., & Inzlicht, M. (2026). People choose to receive human empathy despite rating AI empathy higher. *Communications Psychology, 4*, article 19. https://doi.org/10.1038/s44271-025-00387-3

**Full primary source.** https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00387-3

**Safe teaching claim.** Across four studies, participants preferred to choose a human source for caring responses, yet generally rated the AI responses they experienced as more empathetic and as making them feel more heard. Source preference is not reducible to text ratings. This does not show clinical improvement or that everyone prefers a human.

**Evidence locations.** Methods and Results, Studies 1–4; Figure 1 caption: choice samples 152, 196, 195 and 148, total 691. Study 1 source options use responses from undergraduate assistants or ChatGPT-4o. Study 3 uses curated high-quality human responses, including trained responders. Study 4 uses participants' own life situations, consequential human/AI choices, and later response ratings (140 returned; paired analyses use fewer complete comparisons). Study 4 Results: human chosen 58.4% of trials; 25.68% of participants chose AI in a majority. Therefore avoid “people always choose human.” Human choice percentages across studies are approximately 57–62%, not overwhelming unanimity. General Discussion, “Limitations”: mostly decontextualized empathizers/human strangers; not an established relationship comparison, not repeated live dialogue; positive rating items may miss uncanny/distressing reactions. Source was CHOSEN, not randomized for the rating comparison; choice selection can affect response ratings. All studies are brief, nonclinical tasks, not psychotherapy trials.

**Product inference.** Offer meaningful source/relationship choice rather than assuming the most highly rated language is what users want.

## R9: clinical outcomes require a particular system and support arrangement

**Complete reference.** Heinz, M. V., Mackin, D. M., Trudeau, B. M., Bhattacharya, S., Wang, Y., Banta, H. A., Jewett, A. D., Salzhauer, A. J., Griffin, T. Z., & Jacobson, N. C. (2025). Randomized trial of a generative AI chatbot for mental health treatment. *NEJM AI, 2*(4), AIoa2400802. https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400802

**Access disclosure.** The publisher full-text endpoint returned 403. The primary published article was inspected in full from https://gwern.net/doc/psychiatry/depression/2025-heinz.pdf ; bibliographic identity and trial description were cross-checked with the author's publication record https://www.nicholasjacobson.com/publication/heinz-mackin-trudeau-bhattacharya-wang-et-al-2025/ and institutional research brief https://www.c4tbh.org/research-brief/randomized-trial-of-a-generative-ai-chatbot-for-mental-health-treatment/ . No PDF or publisher figure is committed. Supplementary Appendix and separate author disclosures were not reviewed.

**Safe teaching claim.** A randomized trial of 210 adults with clinically significant depression/anxiety symptoms or high-risk eating-disorder concerns compared an expert-fine-tuned Therabot intervention with a waitlist. The intervention group improved more on symptom scales at four and eight weeks. It was NOT a head-to-head trial against human therapy or a generic consumer companion. All outputs were supervised post-transmission by trained clinicians/researchers, with correction and safety intervention available. Those conditions matter to transfer.

**Evidence locations (PDF positions = printed pages).** p. 1 abstract: 210 adults, 106 intervention/104 waitlist; primary outcomes symptom changes at weeks 4 and 8. p. 3 Trial design/Participants: screened symptom groups, exclusion of active suicidality, mania and psychosis; intervention access continued without daily prompts during weeks 4–8. p. 3 Intervention: expert-curated, primarily CBT dialogues, multiple guardrails and crisis classifier. p. 4: “All responses from Therabot were supervised ... post-transmission”; participant contacted for inappropriate responses or safety concerns. pp. 5–6 Table 1 gives actual symptom outcomes; no need for detailed effect-size numbers on an introductory slide. p. 9 Safety: staff intervened 15 times for safety concerns and 13 times to correct inappropriate chatbot responses. pp. 11–12 Discussion: rapid expert intervention, AI-open selection bias, waitlist differential contact, limited follow-up. The eight-week result is not maintenance after withdrawing access. Alliance comparison is to outpatient norms/historical samples, not concurrent randomized human therapy.

**Teaching consequence.** If lecture time is tight, keep R9 in an appendix as a contrast in study design. If shown in the main lecture, show the supervision and waitlist limitations beside the symptom-outcome statement; avoid “AI therapy is as good as human therapy.”

## Recommended concise main-story selection

- R1: higher perceived response quality does not establish treatment efficacy.
- R2: disclosure/context matter, but the design does not isolate a causal label effect.
- R8: choice of source and quality rating are not the same outcome.
- R6 + R7: distinguish immediate relief from supporting an external human action; preserve measured horizon and comparator.
- R9: optional contrast for stronger outcome evidence, tightly bounded to this supervised system.

The student-team task should ask which users, situations, comparator and outcome support a benefit or risk. None of these papers warrants either a universal pro-AI or anti-AI verdict.
